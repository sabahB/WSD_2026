Week 1 ⏱️ 47 Minutes Total 👥 Group Exercise (4 Students)

Activity: "UX Detective"

🎯 Goal: Identify and analyse frustrating digital experiences using User-Centred Design (UCD) thinking. The aim is to understand the problem rather than design a solution.

📋 Step-by-Step Activity Instructions

Step 1

Individual Reflection — 5 min

Think of a website or app that frustrated you recently. Ask yourself: What happened? What made the experience difficult?

💡 Examples of Digital Frustrations:
  • Couldn't find the "Contact" page.
  • Checkout process was confusing.
  • Too many pop-ups blocking the screen.
  • Navigation menu was unclear or missing key categories.
  • Couldn't understand what to do next.
  • Important information (like return policy or fees) was hidden.

Action: Write your specific example on a sticky note or in your notes.

Step 2

Share & Select — 10 min

In groups of 4:

  1. Share your individual examples with your group members.
  2. Choose one frustration that your group finds most interesting to investigate.
  3. Write the problem as a short statement.
Example Problem Statement: "Users struggle to find the return policy on an online shop."
⚠️ Note: Avoid suggesting a solution at this stage! Focus entirely on defining the friction.
Step 3

Be a UX Detective — 12 min

Investigate the problem deeply from the user's perspective by answering the 5 UX Detective Questions:

  • WHO? Who is experiencing the problem?
  • WHAT? What are they trying to do? (Their core goal)
  • WHERE? Where in the experience does the problem occur?
  • WHY? Why might this be difficult?
  • IMPACT? What happens when the user cannot complete the task easily?
🔍 Worked Example Case Study:
  • User: Online shopper buying clothes
  • Goal: Return a product that doesn't fit
  • Problem: Cannot easily find return policy or instructions on the website
  • Impact: Frustration, wasted time, lost trust, and potentially abandoning the purchase entirely
Step 4

What Would You Need to Know? — 10 min

We think this is a problem—but how could we find out whether it really is? Identify 3 research questions you would want to investigate to gather evidence.

💡 Guidance Questions to Ask:
  • Do other users have the same problem?
  • How do users currently look for this information?
  • Which terminology or button labels do users actually understand?
  • How long does it take users to complete the task?
  • Where on the page do users become confused or click wrongly?
Step 5

Quick Share — 10 min

Each group presents a 1-minute summary using this exact structure:

• Our problem: [Describe the issue]

• Our user: [Describe the user group]

• The user's goal: [What were they trying to accomplish?]

• What we don't know: [Key unknowns or assumptions]

• How we could investigate it: [Research approach]

Week 2 ⏱️ 50 Minutes Total 👥 Group & Peer Exercise

Activity: Research Practice

🎯 Challenge: Designing for Beginner Fitness Customers

📍 Scenario

You are designing a booking and membership website for a local fitness studio catering specifically to beginners.

Your job is not to design the website yet.

Your job is to find out: What might beginner customers need from the service and from the website?

⏱️ 50-Minute Structure

0–5 min

Understand the scenario

Read the scenario. Think about:

  • Who are the users?
  • What might be different about a beginner compared with an experienced gym user?
“Some of your answers are assumptions. Your job is to investigate them.”
5–10 min

What do we need to know?

Each group identify possible research questions.

For example:
  • What concerns do beginners have about joining a fitness studio?
  • What information would help someone decide whether to book a class?
  • What information do beginners want before attending their first session?
  • What might make someone feel more comfortable joining?
  • What types of classes might appeal to beginners?
  • What information do existing fitness studios provide?

A research question tells us what we need to find out.

Research questions

Write 3–5 research questions.

10–25 min

Secondary research

In groups, search for information using reliable online sources.

Find at least two useful sources that help answer one or more of our research questions.

Then complete the table below

For sources, you can use, for instance:
  • NHS
  • Government sources
  • Academic research
  • Professional organisations
  • Fitness industry sources
  • Existing fitness studio websites

Secondary research table

Find useful information from existing sources. Remember: secondary research uses information that has already been collected or published by others.

Research question Source Key finding Why is it useful?
🔍 Check your sources:

Before using information, ask:

  • Who produced the information?
  • Is the source credible?
  • Is it recent enough and relevant to our topic?
  • Does it actually help answer our research question?
25–35 min

Analyse the findings

Now stop searching. Look at what you found and answer:

What does this information tell us about beginner users?

Then:

What might this mean for the service or website?

This is the crucial step. You move from:
Information --> Finding --> Insight --> Possible design implication

From finding to insight

Choose one of your findings.

What did we find? What does this tell us about the users? What might this mean for the service or website?
💡 Example:

Finding: Beginners may be uncertain about what to expect when attending a new fitness class.

Insight: New customers may need reassurance and clear information before booking.

Possible design implication: The website could clearly explain the class, difficulty level, instructor, what happens during the session and what customers should bring.

35–45 min

Primary research

Now, you can conduct primary research with your classmates.

Based on your research, write three questions that you could ask classmates.

For example:
  • Have you ever joined a gym or fitness class?
  • What information would you want to know before attending a class for the first time?
  • What would make you feel more comfortable trying a new fitness class?

Data collection from classmates

Ask your classmates and record some responses.

Question What did people tell us?
45–50 min

Share the insight

Each group gives one finding: “We found that…” and then: “This could mean that…”

For example:
• “We found that beginners can feel uncertain about what to expect from a new fitness class.”
• “This could mean that the website should clearly explain what happens during a first session.”

What did we learn?

“Notice that we didn't start by designing pages. We started by trying to understand the users.”

Week 3 ⏱️ 50 Minutes Total 👥 Group Exercise

Activity – Creating User Personas

🎯 Goal: Translate your Week 2 user research into an evidence-based user persona, moving systematically from user needs to concrete website implications.

📍 Scenario

You are designing a booking and membership website for a local fitness studio catering specifically to beginners.

Last week, you researched potential users and asked your classmates about their experiences, needs and concerns. Today, you will use that research to create a user persona.

📋 Session Steps & Guidance

5 min

Part 1 – Review your research

Look back at your Week 2 research. Identify 3 things you learned about potential users.

💡 Examples:
  • Beginners may be unsure which class is suitable for them.
  • Some people may want to know what happens during their first session.
  • People may want clear information about prices and booking.

⚠️ Important: Try to use findings from your research rather than simply guessing what users might want.

5 min

Part 2 – Choose a user type

Discuss your research findings. Could there be different types of beginners?

💡 Examples of Beginner User Types:
  • A university student who wants to become more active.
  • Someone who has never used a fitness studio before.
  • Someone who wants to improve their fitness but feels nervous about joining.
  • Someone looking for a flexible activity around work or study.

Choose one type of user to represent in your persona.

20 min

Part 3 – Create your persona

Create a persona based on your research findings.

🔍 Worked Example Persona: Meet Sarah

Age: 21

Background: Sarah is a university student who has recently moved to Manchester. She wants to become more active but has never joined a fitness studio before.

Goals: Become more physically active | Find a beginner-friendly activity | Feel comfortable attending her first class

Needs: Clear information about classes | Information about difficulty levels | Information about what to expect

Frustrations / Concerns: Worried that a class might be too difficult | Unsure what to wear or bring | Nervous about attending for the first time

Behaviours: Searches online before choosing a fitness studio | Compares different classes | Looks for information before booking

Website Needs: Clear class descriptions | Beginner-friendly labels | Information about the first visit | Clear booking and pricing information

10 min

Part 4 – Evidence or assumption?

Now check your persona. For each important statement, ask:

"Did our research tell us this, or are we assuming it?"

Mark each statement:

  • R = Supported by research
  • A = Assumption
Example: “Sarah is nervous about attending her first class.”
• If your research showed that beginners have concerns about attending their first class, this could be R.
• If you simply assumed it, it should be A (or rewording to: “Sarah may feel nervous about attending her first class.”).

Why is this important? A persona should be based on evidence about users, not stereotypes or guesses.

10 min

Part 5 – Persona → Website

Look at your persona and identify three important website implications.

Transition Formula:
Week 2: What did we learn about users? --> Week 3: Who are these users? --> Persona: What do they need from our website?

Week 4 ⏱️ 50 Minutes Total 👥 Group Exercise & Analysis

Activity – Competitor Analysis

🎯 Goal: Evaluate existing fitness studio websites through the lens of your persona to identify UI/UX strengths, pain points, and strategic design opportunities for your own site.

📍 Scenario

You are continuing the same project:

  • You are designing a booking and membership website for a local fitness studio catering specifically to beginners.
  • Last week, you created a persona representing one of your potential users.
  • Today, you will look at existing fitness websites and analyse them from your persona's perspective.

📋 Session Steps & Guidance

10 min

Part 1 – The 60-second challenge

Choose one fitness studio or gym website. Imagine you are your persona. You have 60 seconds to find:

⏱️ 60-Second Target Info:
  • What classes are available?
  • How much does it cost?
  • How do you book?
  • Where is it located?
  • Is it suitable for beginners?
🗣️ Discuss in your group:
  • What was easy to find?
  • What was difficult to find?
  • What information was missing?
  • How might this affect your persona?
20 min

Part 2 – Analyse two competitors

Choose two relevant fitness websites and compare them from your persona's perspective.

⚠️ Think about your persona: For each website, ask: “Would our persona be able to easily find what they need?”

15 min

Part 3 – From observation to opportunity

Now identify what you can learn from the competitors. Move beyond just listing features to extracting actionable opportunities.

🔍 Worked Example: Observation --> Opportunity

Observation: A competitor clearly explains the difficulty level of each class.

Why does this matter? Our persona is a beginner and wants to know whether a class is suitable.

Opportunity: Our website could clearly label classes as Beginner, Beginner–Intermediate, or Intermediate, and clearly explain what each level means.

5 min

Part 4 – Share

Each group shares one useful finding using this structure:

• “We found that…”

• “This could mean that our website should…”
